1. Understanding Employee Benefits
What are employee benefits?

Employee benefits are non-wage compensations provided to employees in addition to their salary. Common benefits include health insurance, dental and vision coverage, retirement plans (like 401(k)), life and disability insurance, paid time off, and flexible spending accounts.

Some benefits, like Social Security contributions and unemployment insurance, are legally required of all employers. Others, like health insurance or retirement matching, are offered voluntarily to attract and retain talent. A competitive benefits package can significantly impact employee satisfaction, productivity, and retention.

What benefits are employers legally required to offer?

Every employer pays Social Security and Medicare (FICA) and federal and state unemployment taxes, and most must carry workers' comp (Florida at 4 or more employees, or 1 in construction; Georgia at 3 or more). Health coverage is not legally required, but employers with 50 or more full-time equivalents can owe an ACA penalty if they do not offer it.

Beyond those rules, some states have additional mandates, for example paid family leave or short-term disability. A licensed benefits broker can help you understand which requirements apply to your business based on size, location, and industry.

What is a total compensation package?

Total compensation is the full value an employer provides to an employee: salary plus the dollar value of all benefits. This typically includes employer contributions toward health insurance premiums, retirement plan matching, paid time off, life and disability insurance, and any other perks or allowances.

Many employees underestimate the value of their benefits. Helping employees understand their total compensation, not just their paycheck, is a powerful retention and satisfaction tool. Employers often use total compensation statements during annual reviews or open enrollment to illustrate this value.

2. Employer Health Insurance Basics
How much does employer health insurance cost on average?

Health insurance costs vary widely based on plan type, location, employee demographics, and the employer contribution level. According to KFF's 2025 survey, the average yearly premium for employer coverage was $9,325 for single coverage and $26,993 for family coverage. Employers paid about 84% of single premiums and 74% of family premiums on average.

The right strategy, including plan design, carrier selection, and funding arrangements like level funding, can still change these numbers. A broker's role is to find the best value for your specific workforce.

How many employees do I need to offer group health insurance?

You generally need at least one eligible employee who is not an owner or an owner's spouse. Many carriers will write a small group with just one or two enrolled employees, and most require a minimum share of eligible employees to enroll. Owners with no employees usually look at individual coverage instead.

Employers with 50 or more full-time equivalents can owe an ACA penalty if they do not offer coverage. Smaller employers are not required to offer health insurance, but many still do it for recruiting and retention, and small group plans may qualify for tax credits.

What is the employer tax benefit for offering health insurance?

Employer contributions toward employee health insurance premiums are fully tax-deductible as a business expense. Additionally, when employee contributions are made through a Section 125 cafeteria plan, those contributions are made pre-tax, which saves both the employer and the employee on FICA (Social Security and Medicare) taxes.

Small employers with fewer than 25 full-time equivalent employees may also qualify for the Small Business Health Care Tax Credit, which can cover up to 50% of premium costs. The credit lasts for two years in a row, requires coverage bought through SHOP, and requires you to pay at least half of employee-only premiums. For 2026, average wages must be under about $68,200.

A licensed health insurance broker helps employers design, shop, and manage their benefits programs. The broker evaluates an employer's workforce needs, compares carriers and plan options, negotiates rates, and assists with enrollment, employee education, and ongoing compliance support.

Brokers typically earn commissions from insurance carriers when a plan is sold, meaning their services often come at no direct cost to the employer. A good broker provides year-round support, not just at renewal, including help with claims issues, law changes, and benefit strategy. 4. Health Plan Types
What is the difference between an HMO and a PPO plan?

An HMO (Health Maintenance Organization) requires members to use doctors and hospitals within the plan's network. Members typically select a primary care physician who coordinates their care and provides referrals to specialists. HMOs generally have lower premiums and out-of-pocket costs but offer no coverage for out-of-network care (except emergencies).

A PPO (Preferred Provider Organization) gives members more flexibility. They pay less when using in-network providers but the plan still covers a portion of costs for out-of-network care. No referrals are needed to see specialists. PPOs typically have higher premiums but are preferred by employees who want provider choice or have existing specialist relationships.

What is an EPO plan?

An EPO (Exclusive Provider Organization) is a hybrid between an HMO and a PPO. Like an HMO, it generally requires members to use in-network providers. There is typically no out-of-network coverage except in emergencies. Like a PPO, no primary care physician or referrals are usually required to see a specialist.

EPOs often offer lower premiums than PPOs while providing more flexibility than HMOs. They are a good middle-ground option for employers looking to balance cost control with employee convenience.

What is a High Deductible Health Plan (HDHP)?

A High Deductible Health Plan (HDHP) is a health plan with higher deductibles and lower premiums than traditional plans. To qualify as an HDHP under IRS rules for 2026, the plan must have a minimum deductible of $1,700 for self-only coverage or $3,400 for family coverage, and out-of-pocket maximums no higher than $8,500 (self-only) or $17,000 (family).

The key advantage of an HDHP is that it makes employees eligible to contribute to a Health Savings Account (HSA), a triple-tax-advantaged account. HDHPs paired with employer HSA contributions have become a popular cost-control strategy for employers while still providing strong value to employees.

5. Open Enrollment & Plan Elections
What is open enrollment for employee benefits?

Open enrollment is the designated period each year when employees can enroll in, change, or waive their benefit elections for the upcoming plan year. During this window, employees can sign up for health, dental, vision, FSA, and other benefits; switch between plan options; or add/remove dependents.

Open enrollment is typically 2 to 4 weeks long and occurs before the new plan year begins (often in the fall for January 1 effective dates). After open enrollment closes, changes are generally locked in for the remainder of the plan year unless a qualifying life event occurs. Employers should use this time to clearly communicate plan options, costs, and any changes from the prior year.

What happens if an employee misses open enrollment?

If an employee misses the open enrollment window without experiencing a qualifying life event, they generally cannot enroll in or change their benefits until the next open enrollment period. This can leave an employee without coverage for the entire plan year if they were not already enrolled.

Employers should communicate open enrollment dates clearly and in advance, ideally through multiple channels (email, posters, meetings). Some employers offer a brief grace period, but this is not required and can create compliance complications under a Section 125 cafeteria plan.

6. Qualifying Life Events & Section 125 Rules
Can employees change health insurance elections mid-year?

In most cases, no. Once enrolled under a Section 125 cafeteria plan, elections are locked in for the plan year. Federal tax rules (§1.125-4) allow mid-year changes only if the employee experiences a qualifying life event (QLE) and the requested change is consistent with that event.

Common QLEs include marriage, divorce, birth or adoption of a child, death of a dependent, or loss/gain of other coverage. The change request must typically be submitted within 30 days of the qualifying event. Without a qualifying event, benefit elections generally cannot be changed until the next open enrollment.

What is a qualifying life event for health insurance?

A qualifying life event (QLE) is a significant change in an employee's personal or family situation that allows them to change their benefits outside of open enrollment. Common QLEs include marriage or divorce; birth, adoption, or placement of a child; death of a dependent; change in a spouse's employment (gain or loss of coverage); and loss of other health coverage.

The key principle is that the requested change must be consistent with the event. For example, a birth allows the employee to add the newborn, not switch to a different plan type for unrelated reasons. Requests must also be submitted within the plan's designated deadline (usually 30 days) to be valid.

HR should give employees a clear way to report life events promptly, ideally through a benefits administration platform that records the event date and the time of the request. Reminders in the handbook, during onboarding, and at life milestones help people report the event before the deadline.

Is marriage a qualifying life event?

Yes. Getting married is a qualifying life event that typically allows the employee to add their new spouse to the health plan, switch from individual to family coverage, or in some cases enroll for the first time if they had previously waived coverage. The change must be requested within the plan's deadline, commonly 30 days from the date of marriage.

Marriage may also allow changes to other benefits (dental, vision, FSA elections) depending on the plan document's rules. The election change must always be consistent. For example, moving from single to family health coverage is appropriate after a marriage. Switching to a completely different plan type for unrelated reasons generally is not.

How long do employees have to add a newborn to insurance?

Under HIPAA special enrollment rules, the plan must allow at least 30 days from the date of birth for the employee to add the newborn. If enrolled within this window, coverage is retroactive to the child's date of birth, so there is no gap in coverage. Most employer plans follow this 30-day rule; some state laws or plans may allow up to 60 days.

If the employee misses the deadline, the child cannot be added until the next open enrollment period. Employers should communicate this deadline to new parents proactively, ideally as soon as the birth is reported, since a missed window can result in the child being uninsured for months.

Can an employee drop coverage if their spouse gets insurance elsewhere?

Yes. Gaining other coverage, such as through a spouse's new employer, is a qualifying event that may allow an employee to drop their current employer coverage mid-year. This is permitted under the "gain of other coverage" provision, which many Section 125 plan documents include as an allowable election change event.

The employee must notify the employer in writing within the plan's deadline (often 30 days) and typically must provide proof of the new coverage. Employers should verify their specific plan document allows this scenario, as it must be explicitly included to be valid.

Do employers need a written Section 125 plan document?

Yes. To allow employees to pay for benefits with pre-tax dollars, an employer must maintain a written Section 125 cafeteria plan document. This is a firm IRS requirement. Without it, the plan could be disqualified, making all employee contributions fully taxable and potentially triggering back taxes and penalties.

The written plan document specifies which benefits are offered, the rules for elections and mid-year changes, and the plan year dates. If your company does not already have one, or has not updated it recently, you should create or review it before the next plan year.

7. ERISA Plan Documents & Compliance
What documents are required under ERISA for health plans?

Under ERISA, employers must maintain a written plan document for any employee welfare benefit plan (such as group health insurance) and provide a Summary Plan Description (SPD) to all participants. They must also issue a Summary of Material Modifications (SMM) when significant changes are made. Form 5500 is generally due for plans with 100 or more participants at the start of the year. Smaller plans are usually exempt if they are fully insured, or self-funded and paid from the employer's general assets.

Insurance carrier booklets typically do not contain all required ERISA-specific provisions. That is why most employers use a "wrap plan document" that incorporates the carrier documents and adds the missing ERISA language. Failure to maintain required documents can result in significant DOL penalties.

What is a wrap plan document?

A wrap plan document is a comprehensive ERISA plan document that "wraps around" insurance carrier policy booklets or certificates. Carrier documents typically describe covered benefits but often omit required ERISA provisions such as plan administration details, claims and appeals procedures, participant rights, and plan amendment/termination language.

The wrap document fills in those gaps by incorporating all carrier documents by reference and adding the ERISA-required language. Together, the wrap document and the carrier documents form one complete, compliant ERISA plan document. Do not assume your carrier booklet alone satisfies ERISA. For most employers, it does not.

What is a Summary Plan Description (SPD)?

An SPD is a plain-language document that explains the key terms of the benefit plan to employees. It must describe who is eligible, what benefits are covered, how to file a claim, what the appeals process looks like, and what rights participants have under ERISA. ERISA requires employers to provide an SPD to participants when they first become covered (generally within 90 days of enrollment) and upon request.

If the plan changes significantly, an employer must either issue an updated SPD or a Summary of Material Modifications (SMM) notifying participants of the changes. Many employers include the SPD as part of their wrap plan document package.

Do small employers need ERISA plan documents?

Yes. ERISA requirements for written plan documents and SPDs apply to any private-sector employer sponsoring an employee welfare benefit plan, regardless of company size. A small business with five employees offering group health insurance still needs a compliant plan document and SPD.

Government and most church plans are not covered by ERISA. Form 5500 is generally due for plans with 100 or more participants at the start of the year. Smaller plans are usually exempt if they are fully insured, or self-funded and paid from the employer's general assets. The written plan document and SPD still apply to every private-sector employer, no matter how small. Many small employers do not know that, so a DOL audit can catch them off guard.

What are the penalties for not having ERISA plan documents?

If a participant asks in writing for plan documents and does not get them within 30 days, a court can award up to $110 a day. Separately, the DOL can charge up to $195 a day, up to $1,956 per request, when it asks for documents and does not get them.

Beyond financial penalties, failing to maintain proper plan documents can expose employers to participant lawsuits, since ERISA gives participants the right to sue for benefits when plan terms are unclear or improperly administered. The cost of getting documents in order far outweighs the risk of ignoring them.

8. ACA Employer Mandate & Reporting
What is the ACA employer mandate?

The ACA employer mandate, formally called the Employer Shared Responsibility provision, requires Applicable Large Employers (ALEs) to offer affordable, minimum-value health coverage to their full-time employees and their dependent children, or potentially pay a penalty. ALEs are employers with 50 or more full-time equivalent employees.

The mandate has two penalty tracks: a "no offer" penalty if the employer fails to offer coverage to at least 95% of full-time employees, and an "affordability" penalty if coverage is offered but is unaffordable or does not meet minimum value. Either penalty is only triggered if at least one employee receives a premium tax credit through the ACA Marketplace.

Who is considered a full-time employee under the ACA?

Under the ACA, a full-time employee is generally someone working an average of 30 or more hours per week (or 130 hours or more per month). Part-time employees are counted as fractions of full-time equivalents when determining whether an employer is an ALE. For example, two employees each working 15 hours per week equal one full-time equivalent.

For variable-hour employees, employers can use an IRS-approved look-back measurement period to determine full-time status. Accurate tracking of hours is critical, both for determining ALE status and for making sure the right employees are offered coverage.

What is the ACA affordability rule for 2026?

For plan years beginning in 2026, the ACA affordability percentage is 9.96%. This means an employee's required premium contribution for the lowest-cost, minimum-value self-only plan option cannot exceed 9.96% of their household income. Otherwise the coverage is considered "unaffordable" and may trigger a penalty if the employee seeks a Marketplace subsidy instead.

Because employers rarely know an employee's actual household income, the IRS offers three safe harbors: the W-2 safe harbor (9.96% of Box 1 wages), the rate-of-pay safe harbor (9.96% of monthly wages based on hourly rate × 130 hours), and the federal poverty line safe harbor (a fixed monthly dollar amount, approximately $129.89 for 2026). Using any of these safe harbors protects the employer from the affordability penalty.

What are the ACA penalties for employers in 2026?

There are two ACA penalty amounts for 2026, both of which increased significantly from 2025. The "no offer" penalty (§4980H(a)), triggered when an ALE fails to offer coverage to at least 95% of full-time employees and at least one employee receives a Marketplace subsidy, is $3,340 per full-time employee (minus the first 30). The "unaffordable/insufficient value" penalty (§4980H(b)), triggered when coverage is offered but is unaffordable or below minimum value, is $5,010 per employee who receives a subsidy. The unaffordable penalty can never exceed what the no-offer penalty would have been.

These penalties are indexed annually. The IRS is actively enforcing the employer mandate and has been issuing penalty assessments through Letter 226-J for tax years going back several years. Employers now get at least 90 days to respond to a Letter 226-J, and the IRS has six years to assess a penalty. Working with a broker to stay in compliance is a smart step.

What is the ACA waiting period rule?

Under the ACA, employers cannot impose a waiting period longer than 90 calendar days before health coverage begins. Coverage must be effective no later than the 91st day after an employee becomes eligible.

Note that many carriers do not offer a 90-day waiting period. Most carriers operate on a "First of the Month Following" (FOMF) rule. Best practice waiting periods include: Day of Hire (DOH), FOMF after date of hire, FOMF after 30 days, or FOMF after 60 days. A waiting period of FOMF after 60 days will always stay within the 90-day ACA limit regardless of when in the month the employee is hired.

What ACA reporting forms must employers file?

Applicable Large Employers must file Forms 1094-C and 1095-C with the IRS annually to report offers of coverage made to full-time employees. Form 1095-C must also be furnished to each full-time employee. Employers can now give Form 1095-C only on request, if they post a clear notice that employees can ask for it. These forms tell the IRS whether the employer offered compliant coverage, which the IRS uses to check penalty liability and employees' premium tax credits.

Deadlines change each year. Electronic filing is required for employers submitting 10 or more returns. Penalties for failure to file or furnish correct forms can reach hundreds of dollars per form, with higher penalties for intentional disregard.

9. COBRA Continuation Coverage
What is COBRA continuation coverage?

COBRA is a federal law (the Consolidated Omnibus Budget Reconciliation Act) that allows eligible employees and their covered dependents to continue their employer-sponsored health coverage after a qualifying event causes them to lose that coverage. COBRA ensures temporary continuation of the same group health plan benefits they had before the event.

COBRA is not free. Individuals pay the full cost of coverage (both the employee and employer share) plus up to a 2% administrative fee. During a disability extension, the cost can go up to 150%. This makes COBRA significantly more expensive than the payroll-deducted premium the employee paid while employed, but it preserves access to the same network and plan.

What events trigger COBRA eligibility?

For the covered employee: termination of employment for any reason other than gross misconduct, or reduction in hours below the threshold for coverage eligibility.

For the spouse and dependents: any of the above events, plus the employee becoming entitled to Medicare, divorce or legal separation from the employee, or death of the covered employee.

For dependent children only: loss of dependent status under the plan (such as turning 26 and aging off a parent's plan).

Each of these events gives the affected individual, called a "qualified beneficiary," an independent right to elect COBRA continuation of the same coverage they had before the event.

How long does COBRA coverage last?

Standard COBRA coverage lasts up to 18 months following a termination of employment or reduction of hours. However, the duration can be extended in certain circumstances: up to 36 months if a second qualifying event occurs (such as divorce or death of the employee during the initial 18-month period); up to 29 months if a qualified beneficiary is determined to be disabled within the first 60 days of COBRA.

COBRA coverage can also end earlier than the maximum duration if the individual fails to pay premiums, becomes covered under another group health plan, or becomes entitled to Medicare.

Which employers must offer COBRA?

Federal COBRA applies to private-sector employers and state/local governments that employed 20 or more employees on more than half of the business days in the prior calendar year. Both full-time and part-time employees count toward this threshold (with part-timers counted as fractions of full-time).

Federal COBRA does not apply to federal government plans, church plans, or certain church-affiliated organizations. In Florida, employers with fewer than 20 employees on insured plans fall under state continuation: up to 18 months, at up to 115% of the premium, handled by the carrier. In Georgia, people covered for at least 6 months can continue for the rest of the month plus 3 more months, and people 60 or older may be able to continue longer. State continuation does not apply to self-funded plans.

When must COBRA election notices be sent?

When a qualifying event occurs, the employer has 30 days to notify the plan administrator. The plan administrator then has 14 days to send an election notice to each qualified beneficiary, for a total of up to 44 days from the qualifying event. If the employer is also the plan administrator, the notice must be sent within 44 days.

Once the election notice is received, qualified beneficiaries have 60 days to elect COBRA coverage. Missing the employer's notification deadline can expose the company to significant penalties. Many employers use a third-party COBRA administrator to ensure these timelines are met.

10. HIPAA Special Enrollment Rights
What is HIPAA special enrollment?

HIPAA special enrollment gives employees (and their dependents) the right to enroll in group health coverage outside of open enrollment when they experience certain life events. Unlike open enrollment, these rights are federally mandated and cannot be denied by the employer or insurer.

HIPAA special enrollment triggers include: loss of other health coverage, addition of a new dependent (birth, adoption, or placement for adoption), marriage, and in some cases gaining or losing Medicaid or CHIP eligibility. Employees generally have 30 days from the event to request enrollment (60 days for Medicaid or CHIP events). Coverage for a birth, adoption, or placement starts on the date of the event. For marriage or loss of other coverage, coverage starts no later than the first of the month after the plan gets the request.

What is the difference between a HIPAA special enrollment right and a Section 125 qualifying life event?

They are related but governed by different laws. HIPAA special enrollment rights (under HIPAA's portability provisions) give employees the right to enroll in coverage. The plan cannot deny them entry. Section 125 qualifying life event rules (under IRS tax regulations) govern whether an employee can change their pre-tax benefit elections mid-year without losing the tax benefits of the cafeteria plan.

In practice, most events that trigger HIPAA special enrollment also qualify as a Section 125 QLE. However, an employer should ensure their plan document explicitly lists the relevant QLEs. HIPAA rights are automatic, but Section 125 election changes must be spelled out in the written plan.

11. HSAs, FSAs & Tax-Advantaged Accounts
What is a Health Savings Account (HSA)?

An HSA is a tax-advantaged savings account that employees can use to pay for qualified medical expenses. To be eligible to contribute to an HSA, an employee must be enrolled in a qualifying High Deductible Health Plan (HDHP) and have no other disqualifying health coverage.

HSAs offer a rare "triple tax advantage": contributions are made pre-tax (or tax-deductible), the money grows tax-free, and withdrawals for qualified medical expenses are tax-free. Unlike FSAs, HSA funds roll over indefinitely from year to year and are owned by the employee. They keep the account even if they leave the company. This makes HSAs a powerful long-term healthcare savings tool, not just a short-term spending account. Starting in 2026, employees can still contribute if their plan covers telehealth before the deductible, or if they have a direct primary care membership costing up to $150 a month ($300 for a family).

What are the HSA contribution limits for 2026?

For 2026, the IRS HSA contribution limits are $4,400 for self-only HDHP coverage and $8,750 for family coverage. Individuals age 55 or older may make an additional $1,000 catch-up contribution, bringing their maximums to $5,400 (self-only) or $9,750 (family). These limits include both employee and employer contributions combined.

Employer contributions to employee HSAs are tax-deductible for the employer and tax-free to the employee. Many employers contribute a seed amount to employee HSAs as an incentive for HDHP enrollment. This strategy can help offset the higher deductible employees face while still reducing overall plan costs.

What is a Health Care FSA and how does it work?

A Health Care Flexible Spending Account (FSA) is an employer-sponsored account that allows employees to set aside pre-tax dollars for qualified medical, dental, and vision expenses. For 2026, the annual contribution limit is $3,400, up from $3,300 in 2025. Employees elect their contribution amount during open enrollment and cannot change it during the year without a qualifying event.

A key FSA feature: the full annual election amount is available on day one of the plan year, even though the employee has not yet contributed that amount through payroll. However, unlike HSAs, FSA funds are "use it or lose it." Amounts not spent by plan year end (plus any grace period) are forfeited. Employers may allow a rollover of up to $680 into the next plan year (the 2026 limit).

What is a Dependent Care FSA?

A Dependent Care FSA (DCFSA) allows employees to set aside pre-tax dollars for eligible dependent care expenses, such as daycare, preschool, after-school programs, and elder care for a qualifying dependent. For 2026, the annual limit increased significantly to $7,500 per household (or $3,750 for married individuals filing separately), up from $5,000. This is the first permanent increase in the Dependent Care FSA limit since 1986, enacted through the One Big Beautiful Bill Act.

Unlike a Health Care FSA, DCFSA funds are only available as they are actually contributed, not up front. Employers offering DCFSAs should be aware that the increased limit may affect annual nondiscrimination testing (the 55% average benefits test), which requires that non-highly-compensated employees receive at least 55% of the average benefits received by highly-compensated employees.

Can an employee have both an HSA and an FSA?

Generally, no. An employee enrolled in an HDHP and contributing to an HSA cannot also have a standard Health Care FSA, because the FSA would provide first-dollar health coverage and disqualify them from HSA eligibility. However, there is an exception: a "Limited Purpose FSA" (LPFSA) that covers only dental and vision expenses is permitted alongside an HSA.

Employees can always have a Dependent Care FSA alongside an HSA. Those two accounts cover entirely different types of expenses and do not conflict with each other.

What is an HRA (Health Reimbursement Arrangement)?

An HRA is an employer-funded account that reimburses employees for qualified medical expenses and, in some cases, individual health insurance premiums. Unlike HSAs and FSAs, HRAs are funded entirely by the employer. Employees cannot contribute to an HRA.

There are several types of HRAs. An ICHRA (Individual Coverage HRA) lets employers of any size reimburse employees for individual health insurance premiums and medical expenses tax-free, and it has no IRS dollar cap. A QSEHRA (Qualified Small Employer HRA) is for employers with fewer than 50 full-time equivalents that do not offer a group health plan. For 2026, a QSEHRA can reimburse up to $6,450 for an individual or $13,100 for a family. HRAs are a practical way to help with health costs without running a traditional group plan.

What is an ICHRA, and is it right for my company?

An ICHRA lets you give employees a set monthly amount, tax free, to buy their own individual health plan. You choose amounts by employee class, like full-time or part-time, with no IRS cap. It can fit groups spread across locations or facing high group rates. Employees must have individual coverage to use it, and you give a notice at least 90 days before the plan year.

What is a QSEHRA, and what are the 2026 limits?

A QSEHRA is for employers with fewer than 50 full-time equivalents that do not offer a group health plan. For 2026 you can reimburse up to $6,450 for an individual or $13,100 for a family, tax free. Employees need their own health coverage, and you give written notice 90 days before the year starts.

What is a Section 128 (Trump Account) contribution?

Starting in 2026, employers can contribute up to $2,500 a year per employee, tax free, to Trump Accounts for the employee or their children, under a written Section 128 plan. The $2,500 is a total per employee, not per child.

12. Level Funding & Reference-Based Pricing
What is level funding and how does it differ from traditional fully insured coverage?

Level funding is a form of self-funded health insurance where the employer pays a fixed ("level") monthly amount, similar to a fully insured premium, but the plan is technically self-funded. That monthly payment covers expected claims, stop-loss insurance (which protects the employer if claims are higher than projected), and administrative fees.

If claims come in lower than expected, the employer may receive a refund at year end, but whether you get one depends on the contract. With a traditional fully insured plan, the carrier keeps any unused premium. Level funding also gives employers their own claims data, which helps with plan design and cost management. Level funding is now common for small businesses. KFF found 37% of covered workers at small firms were in level-funded plans in 2025. Minimum group size depends on the carrier.

What is reference-based pricing?

Reference-based pricing (RBP) is a cost-containment strategy used in self-funded plans where the plan pays claims based on a benchmark price, typically a percentage of Medicare rates, rather than negotiated rates from an insurance carrier network. This gives the plan direct control over how much it pays for each service.

RBP can significantly reduce claims costs compared to traditional carrier-negotiated rates, particularly for hospital and facility services where the spread between Medicare and carrier rates is largest. The tradeoff is that some providers may bill balance amounts above the reference price, requiring a member advocacy program to negotiate on behalf of employees. When implemented well, RBP is one of the most effective cost-reduction tools available to self-funded employers.

What is stop-loss insurance?

Stop-loss insurance protects a self-funded or level-funded employer from very large or unexpected claims. There are two types: specific stop-loss (which limits the employer's liability for any single individual's claims above a set threshold) and aggregate stop-loss (which caps the employer's total claims liability for the entire group in a plan year).

Stop-loss is what makes self-funded and level-funded plans viable for small and mid-size employers. It prevents one very expensive claim (such as a cancer diagnosis or premature birth) from creating financial hardship for the employer. The specific deductible (the per-person threshold) is a key variable in plan design and pricing.

13. Mental Health Parity & Federal Mandates
What is the Mental Health Parity and Addiction Equity Act (MHPAEA)?

The Mental Health Parity and Addiction Equity Act (MHPAEA) requires group health plans that offer mental health or substance use disorder (MH/SUD) benefits to provide those benefits no more restrictively than medical and surgical benefits. This applies to financial requirements (like deductibles and copays) and treatment limitations (like visit limits and prior authorization requirements).

Parity applies to employer plans with more than 50 employees, and to insured small group plans through the ACA. Plans must keep a written comparison showing limits like prior authorization are applied the same way to mental health and medical benefits.

What is the No Surprises Act?

The No Surprises Act, effective January 1, 2022, protects insured patients from unexpected bills for emergency care, non-emergency care at in-network facilities from out-of-network providers, and air ambulance services from out-of-network providers. Under the law, patients in these situations pay only their in-network cost-sharing amounts, and any dispute over the balance is resolved between the payer and provider.

Your plan must keep its provider directory current, show deductibles on ID cards, and offer a price comparison tool online and by phone. Self-funded plans are covered too, so check that your TPA handles these.

What is the Consolidated Appropriations Act (CAA) and what does it require of employers?

The Consolidated Appropriations Act of 2021 (CAA) included several important health plan provisions. Key employer obligations include: gag clause prohibitions (employers cannot enter into contracts that restrict access to provider cost and quality data); broker and consultant compensation disclosure requirements (covered service providers must disclose their compensation to plan fiduciaries); and expanded mental health parity requirements including a comparative analysis of non-quantitative treatment limitations.

Employers also have two yearly filings, usually handled with the carrier or TPA: the gag clause attestation by December 31 and the prescription drug (RxDC) report by June 1.

14. Voluntary Benefits
What are voluntary benefits?

Voluntary benefits are supplemental insurance products offered through the employer but paid for entirely (or mostly) by the employee through payroll deduction. Common voluntary benefits include life insurance, short-term and long-term disability, accident insurance, critical illness insurance, hospital indemnity, legal plans, identity theft protection, and pet insurance.

Although employees pay the premiums, offering voluntary benefits through the workplace provides value: employees get access to group pricing (lower than individual market rates), the convenience of payroll deduction, and, when set up under a Section 125 plan, the ability to pay with pre-tax dollars for qualifying products. For employers, voluntary benefits enhance the overall benefits package at minimal cost.

What is the difference between group life insurance and voluntary life insurance?

Group life insurance is typically employer-paid, providing a base benefit amount (often one to two times annual salary) to all eligible employees at no cost to them. This is a common core benefit offering. Voluntary life insurance allows employees to purchase additional life coverage beyond the employer-provided amount, paid through payroll deduction.

Both types are term life insurance that ends when the employee leaves the company (though employees may have conversion or portability rights to keep individual coverage). Employers should note that employer-paid group life coverage above $50,000 per employee creates imputed income, a taxable benefit, that must be reported on the employee's W-2.

What is short-term vs. long-term disability insurance?

Short-term disability (STD) insurance replaces a portion of an employee's income (typically 60 to 70%) when they are unable to work due to illness, injury, or pregnancy. Coverage usually begins after a short elimination period (1 to 14 days) and lasts for a limited period, commonly 12 to 26 weeks.

Long-term disability (LTD) insurance picks up where short-term disability ends, providing income replacement for longer-term disabilities. LTD benefits typically begin after 90 days and can continue for years, sometimes until retirement age for severe disabilities. Both are important components of a comprehensive benefits package, as a disability is far more likely than most employees realize and can be financially devastating without coverage.

15. Benefits Administration & HR Operations
What is benefits administration software (BenAdmin)?

Benefits administration (BenAdmin) software automates and manages employee benefits enrollment, eligibility tracking, life event changes, and carrier data feeds. Employees can log in to view, enroll in, or change their benefits online rather than through paper forms. HR can manage eligibility, run reports, and track compliance deadlines in one platform.

Modern BenAdmin platforms integrate with payroll systems and send automated eligibility feeds to insurance carriers, reducing manual errors and administrative burden. For employers with 10 or more employees, a BenAdmin platform can save significant time, reduce compliance risk, and improve the employee experience during enrollment.

What is an employee census and why is it needed?

An employee census is a spreadsheet that lists all employees (and often their covered dependents) with demographic information used to quote and price health plans. It typically includes each person's date of birth, gender, hire date, zip code, job classification, and current benefit elections.

Carriers use census data to calculate group rates, particularly age-based and composite-rated premiums. Keeping an accurate, up-to-date census is important for obtaining accurate renewal quotes, budgeting for benefit costs, and ensuring dependents are correctly enrolled. Brokers typically request a current census at each renewal.

16. Required Compliance Notices
What is a Summary of Benefits and Coverage (SBC)?

An SBC is a standardized, plain-language summary that health plans and insurers must provide to participants. It outlines plan coverage in a consistent format, including deductibles, out-of-pocket maximums, covered benefits, and cost-sharing for common services, so employees can compare plans on equal terms.

Under the ACA, employers must distribute the SBC to employees before open enrollment and to new hires at enrollment. Failure to provide an SBC can result in penalties. Your insurance carrier typically provides the SBC template for each plan. Employers are responsible for making sure it goes out on time.

What is the CHIP notice employers must distribute?

The CHIP notice (Children's Health Insurance Program) is a required annual notice that informs employees of potential premium assistance available through their state's Medicaid or CHIP programs. Employers must distribute this notice every year to all employees, not just those enrolled in the health plan, in states that offer premium help. Florida and Georgia both offer premium help, so employers here must send it every year.

The DOL provides a model notice that employers can customize. It is typically sent during open enrollment or with the Summary Plan Description. Not sending it can bring a DOL penalty of up to $145 a day for each employee.

What is the Medicare Part D creditable coverage notice?

Employers must provide an annual notice to all employees and dependents who are eligible for Medicare informing them whether the employer's prescription drug coverage is "creditable" (at least as good as the standard Medicare Part D benefit) or "non-creditable." This matters because Medicare-eligible individuals who delay enrolling in Part D without creditable coverage may face late enrollment penalties.

The notice must be distributed at least once per year, prior to October 15 (the start of Medicare's annual open enrollment), and within specific timeframes for new hires and when plan coverage changes. Your carrier can help you determine whether your plan's drug coverage qualifies as creditable. Employers must also report creditable status to CMS online within 60 days after the plan year starts.

What happens when an employee turns 65 or goes on Medicare?

If you have 20 or more employees, your plan pays first and the employee can usually wait on Medicare Part B without a penalty. Under 20 employees, Medicare usually pays first, so the employee should sign up for Part A and B on time. Check with us before an employee drops group coverage or enrolls in Medicare.

What is the Women's Health and Cancer Rights Act (WHCRA) notice?

The WHCRA requires group health plans that cover mastectomies to also cover reconstructive surgery and related services following a mastectomy. Employers must provide a notice to participants informing them of these rights at the time of enrollment and annually thereafter.

This is one of several notices that employers frequently overlook in their annual compliance checklist. It is typically included in the SPD or the wrap plan document package, but employers should verify their annual distribution includes it.

What compliance reports and filings must employers submit annually?

Key annual compliance filings include: Form 5500 (generally due for plans with 100 or more participants at the start of the year; smaller plans are usually exempt if they are fully insured, or self-funded and paid from the employer's general assets); Forms 1094-C and 1095-C (ACA reporting for ALEs with 50+ FTEs); and the annual CHIP/Medicaid notice, Medicare Part D notice, and SBC distribution.

Self-funded and level-funded plans and HRAs pay the PCORI fee on Form 720 by July 31. Almost every plan also files a gag clause attestation and an RxDC report, usually with help from the carrier or TPA. Other notices may be required based on your plan design, such as the HIPAA Notice of Privacy Practices, the WHCRA notice, and the Newborns' and Mothers' Health Protection Act notice. Maintaining a compliance calendar with deadlines for each required notice and filing is one of the most effective ways to stay out of trouble. Your broker should help you manage this calendar.

Do I have to pay the PCORI fee?

If your plan is self-funded or level-funded, or you offer an HRA, yes. It is a small per-person fee filed on Form 720 by July 31 each year. For fully insured plans, the carrier pays it.
